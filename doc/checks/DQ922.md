# DQ922 share-count-mismatch

**Family:** statements, share counts (DQ92x) · **Severity:** warn by default, from
`p_error` · **Applies to:** statements, with `--items` · **Bayesian** (the share
terms model, [statements.md](../statements.md#share-counts))

## What it reports

A period whose weighted-average shares (income statement) and outstanding shares
(balance sheet) are a clean split ratio or a power of ten apart, which no split on
file explains. The two counts are put into the latest terms first, each with its
own version's `known_from`. Every weighted-average count `--items` lists is
compared with every outstanding count.

Three explanations are weighed:

| Hypothesis | What it means |
|---|---|
| `real` | the period's issuance or buybacks: a weighted average is the period's mean, so it lags an outstanding count that moved |
| `mismatch` | one count stored wrong: in other terms, or at a power of ten (prior 0.01 a period) |
| `unreported_split` | a split missing from the actions between the two counts' knowledge dates, when they differ |

`p_error` is `mismatch`'s probability. A split on file with its ex-date between
the period's start and the later filing explains a mismatch at its ratio. An
example is a split after the period ends that the cover's count already shows.

```
IND02  2019-03-30 Q1 FY2019 income v1  DQ922 error  share-count-mismatch  weighted_average_shares (288506777000) and shares_outstanding (289228979) disagree by x1000, which no split on file explains  (line 473)
```

A mismatch that a split missing from the actions explains folds into DQ921's
report of that split. A restated income statement in the new terms against a
balance sheet in the old is one case.

**Keys.** The income row's version `record_key`, with `item` (the weighted
average). The detail holds `item`, `value`, `other`, `other_value`, `ratio` and
`views`.

**Versions.** Both views are judged: the in-force view pairs the period's rows in
force, which can be versions filed years apart.

## Fix

Re-fetch the period from its filing and compare both counts with it. Report the
vendor's value to the vendor. A count at ×0.001 is often one in thousands where
units were meant.
